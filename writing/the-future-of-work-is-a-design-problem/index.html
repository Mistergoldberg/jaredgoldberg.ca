The Future of Work Is a Design Problem

Many people are not outside the economy because they lack value. They are outside because work is badly wired.

The Job Is Wired

Old Christmas lights had a cruel design flaw.

Every bulb sat on the same circuit. If one connection failed, the entire string went dark.

Most jobs are wired the same way.

Arrive at a fixed time. Navigate the commute. Sustain a full shift.

Move at speed. Switch tasks without losing the thread.

Read managers, coworkers, and customers. Absorb the noise, light, paperwork, hierarchy, and pressure.

Then perform the actual work.

Every requirement sits on one circuit. Break one connection and the whole role goes dark.

Even when the person can do the work.

We call this a job.

But a job is not one thing. It is a task surrounded by conditions, schedules, environments, expectations, and social requirements.

Some are essential. Many are inherited.

Some make the institution easier to operate. Others have simply gone unexamined.

A person may be able to prepare drinks but not sustain an eight-hour shift. Package products but not tolerate a loud room.

Track inventory but not perform for a line of impatient customers. Repeat a task reliably but struggle with constant switching.

The capability is present.

The conditions shut it down.

Statistics Canada's 2022 Canadian Survey on Disability found that nearly one in four adults aged 25 to 64 lives with at least one disability.

This is not an edge case. It is a design problem at labour-market scale.

When the circuit is treated as natural, exclusion looks like incapacity.

But sometimes the person has not failed the job.

The wiring has.

Tasks Are Not the Condition

A task is the work that creates value.

Make the drink. Count the stock. Clean the surface. Design the sign. Track the sale.

A condition is the environment around that work.

The shift. The commute. The lighting. The noise. The interruptions. The public performance.

Tasks and conditions are not the same thing.

Standard employment wires them together, then asks one blunt question:

Can this person tolerate the entire inherited job?

If the answer is no, the system records incapacity. But it has tested the circuit, not the capability.

It has confused one arrangement with the work itself.

This is where design thinking changes the starting point.

Tim Brown and IDEO helped popularize a discipline that sounds obvious and is rarely practised:

Begin with the person.

Stanford's d.school helped turn that principle into a repeatable method.

Watch where friction appears. Understand the constraints. Separate what the system truly needs from what it merely inherited.

Then redesign around reality.

Most institutions begin with the circuit diagram and test people against it. Design thinking begins with the person and asks whether the diagram should change.

What can this person do well? Under what conditions? At what rhythm? In what sequence?

One approach measures conformity.

The other reveals capacity.

Coffee Is Not One Job

A café looks like a counter.

Behind one cup of coffee sits a small economy.

Drinks must be made. Surfaces cleaned. Shelves restocked.

Inventory counted. Suppliers managed. Signs designed.

Sales tracked. Events hosted. Customers welcomed.

A standard café wires all of this into one role. Then it asks one person to be good at everything at once.

Fast. Flexible. Socially fluent. Calm under pressure. Consistent across a full shift.

That model is not inevitable.

It reflects a particular operating logic: speed, standardization, interchangeability, throughput.

Large chains are exceptionally good at that model. Their labour structure mirrors their business model.

But if the goal is not to compete on speed alone, the work can be wired differently.

Customer-facing work can separate from back-of-house work. Fast tasks from steady ones.

Loud environments from quiet ones. Social work from numerical work.

Full shifts from shorter contribution windows.

Each path asks for something different.

Drink preparation rewards sequence and dexterity. Inventory rewards consistency.

Packaging rewards repetition. Signage rewards visual judgment.

Sales tracking rewards comfort with numbers.

The job asks for every strength at once.

The work does not.

That is the difference between accommodation and design.

Accommodation helps someone survive the existing circuit.

Design asks whether everything needed to be on the same circuit in the first place.

Contribution Before Inclusion

Unbundling work does not mean lowering the standard.

The drink must still be correct. The stock must still be counted.

The customer must still receive value.

The standard belongs to the work. The unnecessary conditions do not.

This is where inclusion often fails.

Someone outside the system is invited in. The language becomes softer. The intention becomes kinder.

But the operating model remains untouched.

The person is welcomed into an environment that still does not fit them, then blamed quietly when the mismatch returns.

Inclusion without demand is theatre.

A place in a program is not yet a place in the economy.

Capability Works begins with a harder test:

Does the work create something another person values enough to pay for?

The work must be real. The customer must be real. The income must be earned.

Then participation changes character.

The person is no longer displayed as the beneficiary of a program.

They are known by what they contribute.

That is not charity.

It is economic membership.

The Same Method, Later in Life

The Money Club teaches young people to find friction, follow the money, test demand, and build a response.

Capability Works applies the same method after standardized employment has hardened around adult life.

Observe the person. Isolate the friction. Redesign the work. Test whether capability can become economically real.

One project teaches people to see systems before entering them. The other rewires participation after the system has already excluded too many.

From Circuit to Grid

Real demand creates revenue. Revenue sustains work. Work creates income.

Income creates stability. Stability keeps contribution possible.

Now one circuit closes.

Murray Barr showed the inverse: instability moving from shelter to hospital to police to court, each failure feeding the next.

Capability Works asks whether stability can compound too.

But a café cannot close the employment gap.

Disability is too varied. One workplace offers too few forms of work.

Capability Works becomes civic infrastructure only when the method travels: into offices, retailers, kitchens, warehouses, cultural institutions and remote teams.

A shared layer helps workers define capability. It helps employers unbundle roles. It finances paid trials and accommodations. It keeps support available when a placement—or a person's capacity—changes.

A conventional job with accommodations. A carved role. A shorter shift. Remote work. Supported self-employment. A pause, then re-entry.

Different paths. Same test.

Real demand. Competitive pay. Worker choice. A route forward.

The civic function is not to employ everyone inside one organization.

It is to make more organizations able to employ differently.

The café is the test circuit.

The network is the grid.

Rewire the Job

The future of work is usually discussed as a technology problem.

What will AI automate? Which jobs will disappear? Which skills will command a premium?

Those questions matter.

But a quieter question sits in the wiring:

Why must a person survive six unrelated conditions to prove they can perform one valuable task?

The standard job was not handed down by nature.

It was assembled around the needs of institutions: predictable scheduling, simplified management, standardized roles, consistent throughput, and workers able to absorb the friction the system produced.

That arrangement made organizations easier to operate.

It did not make it the only legitimate form of work.

The future of work does not need more capable people.

The capability is already here. The work is already here.

What fails is the connection between them.

The answer is not to lower the standard.

It is to stop letting one unnecessary condition black out every other capacity.

Rewire that connection and something larger begins to appear.

Not something an institution can announce, award, or bestow.

Something the structure itself produces.
