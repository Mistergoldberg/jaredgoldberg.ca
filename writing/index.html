🧭 Field Guide

A field guide to systems that shape outcomes before you understand them

These essays map patterns that repeat across wealth, media, markets, biology, and belief.

Not arguments.

Instruments.

Ways of looking at familiar systems until they reveal the forces underneath them — compounding, incentives, feedback loops, hierarchy, constraint.

The claim is simple:

Different domains often run on the same underlying logic.

Once you see the pattern, you stop mistaking outcomes for accidents.

📖 Foreword

When Systems Rhyme

I have a habit of squinting at the universe.

By this I mean stepping back until the details soften and the shape comes forward.

At the right distance, things that have no business resembling one another begin to rhyme. A solar system starts to look like a schoolyard. An ant colony begins to resemble a market. A bridge under load begins to resemble a society.

The closer you look, the comparisons become absurd.

Gravity is not popularity. Ants do not have credit scores. Concrete does not feel shame. These systems are made of different things, moved by different forces, and constrained in different ways.

But step back again and certain silhouettes return: centres and edges, currents and bottlenecks, attraction, accumulation, feedback, strain.

This is not a theory of everything.

It is a way of looking at almost anything.

The essays that follow begin with resemblance. They ask what becomes visible when two distant things are placed under the same light.

Sometimes the rhyme survives inspection. Refocus and a shared mechanism appears: compounding, reinforcement, delayed feedback, lost friction.

Sometimes it does not. The causes diverge. The comparison breaks. What looked like one pattern from afar turns out to be two entirely different machines.

That is not failure. Metaphor has still done useful work. It has made the familiar strange enough to examine.

The danger comes only when we forget that we were squinting.

A resemblance is not proof. A recurring pattern is not a universal law. And finding a pattern in nature does not tell us what people ought to build.

So the method here is simple.

Squint.

Notice the shape.

Then open your eyes and inspect the machinery.

Ask what is moving. What accumulates? What reinforces itself? Where does the pressure travel? Who carries the cost? What slows the loop? What breaks it?

This is metaphysics in work clothes. No incense required.

I do not expect the universe to reduce itself to one clean diagram.

But I suspect it leaves echoes.

These essays follow them.

The resemblance is the invitation.

The mechanism is the work.
